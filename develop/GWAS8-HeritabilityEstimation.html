Estimating SNP heritability

NoteImportant notes for this notebook

SNP heritability (\(h^2_{\text{SNP}}\)) is the fraction of the variation in a trait that is explained by all measured SNPs together. It tells us how much a trait can be predicted from genotypes, and it is used as a tuning parameter by the methods in the rest of the course: mixed-model GWAS (GWAS9), fine-mapping (GWAS10) and polygenic scores (GWAS11). In this notebook we estimate \(h^2_{\text{SNP}}\) in two ways: from individual-level genotypes (ldak --reml) and from GWAS summary statistics (ldak --sum-hers).

We use two datasets:

  • Individual-level analyses: a simulated cohort (Data/SIM.QC) of 5,000 unrelated synthetic Europeans genotyped for 50,000 SNPs. Its phenotype (Data/SIM.pheno) was simulated with a true SNP heritability of 0.5, so we can check whether the methods get the right answer.
  • Summary-statistics analysis: the height GWAS (Data/Height.QC.gz) and the European reference panel (Data/EUR.QC) from GWAS7.

Learning outcomes

  • Define SNP heritability (\(h^2_{\text{SNP}}\)) and explain why it limits how well a trait can be predicted from SNPs
  • Compare the assumptions of the GCTA (\(\alpha = -1\)) and LDAK (\(\alpha = -0.25\)) heritability models
  • Estimate SNP heritability from individual-level data using REML (ldak --reml)
  • Estimate SNP heritability from GWAS summary statistics using SumHer (ldak --sum-hers), and explain how LD tagging separates true heritability from confounding

NoteSome theory

Intuition First: How can we estimate heritability without knowing which SNPs are causal?

In GWAS5, most SNPs had effects far too small to reach genome-wide significance (\(p < 5 \times 10^{-8}\)). Together, however, they can explain a large part of the trait variance. There are two ways to estimate this total without knowing which SNPs are causal:

  • From individual-level data (REML): Even “unrelated” people share slightly more or slightly fewer alleles than average, purely by chance. If a trait is heritable, pairs of people who are a little more similar genetically will, on average, also be a little more similar in their phenotype. REML measures how strongly phenotypic similarity follows genetic similarity.
  • From GWAS summary statistics (SumHer): A SNP in a region of strong linkage disequilibrium (LD) is correlated with (“tags”) many neighbouring SNPs, so it is more likely to tag a causal variant. For a polygenic trait, SNPs that tag more of the genome therefore have larger GWAS \(\chi^2\) statistics on average. How quickly \(\chi^2\) rises with the amount of tagging tells us the heritability.

Formal Definition of SNP Heritability (\(h^2_{\text{SNP}}\))

We model a quantitative phenotype \(y\) of \(N\) individuals using all \(M\) SNPs at once:

\[y = Z\gamma + \sum_{j=1}^{M} X_j b_j + \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_e^2 I)\]

  • \(Z\gamma\): the covariates, such as sex and principal components
  • \(X_j\): the genotypes at SNP \(j\) (0, 1 or 2 copies of allele A1); \(f_j\) is its minor allele frequency (MAF)
  • \(b_j\): the effect of one copy of the allele

The total genetic value \(g = \sum_{j} X_j b_j\) is treated as a random effect with variance \(\sigma_g^2\). The SNP heritability is the fraction of the phenotypic variance that it explains:

\[h^2_{\text{SNP}} = \frac{\sigma_g^2}{\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_e^2}\]

\(h^2_{\text{SNP}}\) only includes genetic variation tagged by the genotyped SNPs, so it is usually smaller than the narrow-sense heritability \(h^2\) estimated from twin or family studies.

The Heritability Model: How does heritability vary across SNPs?

If the phenotype has variance 1, SNP \(j\) explains \(h_j^2 = 2 f_j (1 - f_j) b_j^2\) of it (under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, \(2 f_j (1 - f_j)\) is the variance of \(X_j\)). Every method has to assume how much heritability each SNP is expected to explain. The LDAK heritability model (Speed et al., 2012; Speed et al., 2017) writes this as:

\[\mathbb{E}[h_j^2] \propto \left[ 2 f_j (1 - f_j) \right]^{1 + \alpha} \times w_j\]

  • \(\alpha\) (the MAF power) describes how heritability depends on allele frequency. With \(\alpha = -1\), the bracket disappears and every SNP is expected to explain the same amount, which means rare SNPs must have much larger per-allele effects. With \(\alpha = 0\), all SNPs have the same expected per-allele effect, so common SNPs explain much more. Human traits typically fit best in between (\(\alpha \approx -0.25\)): rare variants tend to have larger per-allele effects (natural selection keeps large-effect variants rare), but each common SNP still explains more heritability.
  • \(w_j\) (the LD weight) prevents regions full of correlated SNPs from being counted many times. Analogy: if 5 witnesses saw the same event and tell the same story, counting them as 5 independent witnesses overstates the evidence 5-fold. In the same way, 5 SNPs in near-perfect LD carry the information of one SNP, so the LDAK-Thin model keeps only one of them.
Heritability Model MAF Power (\(\alpha\)) LD Weight (\(w_j\)) Key Assumption
GCTA Model \(\alpha = -1\) \(w_j = 1\) (none) Every SNP is expected to explain the same heritability, whatever its MAF or LD. Regions with many SNPs in LD therefore get more heritability.
LDAK-Thin Model \(\alpha = -0.25\) \(w_j = 0\) or \(1\): near-duplicate SNPs (\(r^2 > 0.98\)) are removed Common SNPs explain more heritability than rare SNPs, and each group of near-duplicate SNPs is counted once. We use this model throughout the course.
BLD-LDAK Model \(\alpha = -0.25\) Continuous LD weights + functional annotations (66 parameters; Speed et al., 2020) Also lets coding, conserved and regulatory regions explain more heritability. Fits GWAS summary statistics best, but needs annotation files.

Why Heritability Matters for the Rest of the Course

  1. Mixed-model GWAS (GWAS9): Step 1 uses \(\hat{h}^2_{\text{SNP}}\) to decide how much of the phenotype to explain by the polygenic background.
  2. Fine-mapping (GWAS10): SuSiE needs a prior for the size of causal effects, which is a per-locus version of the same idea (SuSiE estimates it from the data).
  3. Polygenic scores (GWAS11): \(\hat{h}^2_{\text{SNP}}\) and the per-SNP heritabilities \(\mathbb{E}[h_j^2]\) decide how strongly noisy GWAS effect estimates are shrunk towards zero.

Quick Concept Check (from Lecture 8): - A trait has twin heritability \(h^2 = 80\%\) and SNP heritability \(h^2_{\text{SNP}} = 45\%\). What is the highest \(R^2\) a polygenic score built from these SNPs can reach? \(45\%\), because SNPs can only predict the variation that they tag (\(R^2 \le h^2_{\text{SNP}}\)). - Why do we run ldak --thin before computing the relatedness matrix \(K\)? Without thinning, a block of 5 identical SNPs would be counted 5 times.

Individual-Level Heritability Estimation (ldak --reml)

With individual-level genotypes and phenotypes, we estimate \(h^2_{\text{SNP}}\) in two steps: 1. Compute the \(N \times N\) genetic relatedness matrix (GRM) \(K\), which records how genetically similar each pair of individuals is (ldak --calc-kins-direct). 2. Use restricted maximum likelihood (REML) to find the genetic variance \(\sigma_g^2\) and residual variance \(\sigma_e^2\) that best explain the phenotypes, assuming \(y \sim \mathcal{N}(Z\gamma, \sigma_g^2 K + \sigma_e^2 I)\) (ldak --reml). In words: how much of the similarity between people’s phenotypes is explained by their genetic similarity?

Switch to the Bash kernel.

First, let’s link the Data folder, create Results/GWAS8, and remove the header lines from the simulated phenotype (Data/SIM.pheno) and the covariates (Data/SIM.covariate: sex and the top 6 principal components), because LDAK expects files without headers:

ln -sf ../Data
mkdir -p Results/GWAS8

# Strip header lines from phenotype and covariate files for compatibility
awk 'NR>1 {print $1, $2, $3}' Data/SIM.pheno > Results/GWAS8/SIM.pheno
awk 'NR>1 {print $0}' Data/SIM.covariate > Results/GWAS8/SIM.covar

echo "Individuals with phenotypes: $(wc -l < Results/GWAS8/SIM.pheno)"
head -n 5 Results/GWAS8/SIM.pheno
head -n 5 Results/GWAS8/SIM.covar

Now let’s compute the GRM and run REML under two heritability models: the GCTA model (--power -1, no LD weights) and the LDAK-Thin model (remove near-duplicate SNPs with --thin, then --power -0.25).

Key LDAK options used below: - --thin <prefix>: Removes near-duplicate SNPs (\(r^2 > 0.98\) within 100 kb) and writes the list of SNPs to keep to <prefix>.in. - --calc-kins-direct <prefix>: Computes the \(N \times N\) GRM from the PLINK files given by --bfile. - --power <alpha>: Sets the MAF power \(\alpha\) (-1 for the GCTA model, -0.25 for the LDAK model). - --weights <file> / --ignore-weights YES: Use the SNP weights in <file> (here 1 for kept SNPs, 0 for removed ones), or give every SNP weight 1. - --reml <prefix>: Runs REML using the GRM (--grm), phenotype (--pheno) and covariates (--covar).

# Note: this step takes about 1-2 minutes to run.
# 1. GCTA Model (power = -1, uniform expected heritability per SNP)
ldak --calc-kins-direct Results/GWAS8/kins_gcta \
     --bfile Data/SIM.QC \
     --ignore-weights YES \
     --power -1

ldak --reml Results/GWAS8/reml_gcta \
     --grm Results/GWAS8/kins_gcta \
     --pheno Results/GWAS8/SIM.pheno \
     --covar Results/GWAS8/SIM.covar
# Note: this step takes about 1-2 minutes to run.
# 2. LDAK-Thin Model (thin duplicate SNPs with r^2 > 0.98, then compute GRM with power = -0.25)
ldak --thin Results/GWAS8/thin \
     --bfile Data/SIM.QC \
     --window-prune 0.98 \
     --window-kb 100

awk '{print $1, 1}' Results/GWAS8/thin.in > Results/GWAS8/weights.thin

ldak --calc-kins-direct Results/GWAS8/kins_ldak \
     --bfile Data/SIM.QC \
     --weights Results/GWAS8/weights.thin \
     --power -0.25

ldak --reml Results/GWAS8/reml_ldak \
     --grm Results/GWAS8/kins_ldak \
     --pheno Results/GWAS8/SIM.pheno \
     --covar Results/GWAS8/SIM.covar

Summary-Statistics Heritability Estimation (ldak --sum-hers)

In unrelated individuals, the standard error of a REML estimate is roughly \(316/N\) (Visscher et al., 2014): about \(0.06\) for our \(N = 5,000\) cohort, but about \(0.65\) for the \(N \approx 480\) individuals in Data/EUR.QC. Precise REML estimates therefore need thousands of genotyped individuals. Large GWAS usually share only summary statistics, but SumHer (ldak --sum-hers) can estimate \(h^2_{\text{SNP}}\) from these, together with an LD reference panel. Here we use the height GWAS Data/Height.QC.gz (\(N = 388,028\)).

SumHer is based on the expected \(\chi^2\) statistic of each SNP \(j\):

\[\mathbb{E}[\chi_j^2] \approx 1 + N \sum_{k} r_{jk}^2 \, \mathbb{E}[h_k^2]\]

In words: on average, a SNP’s \(\chi^2\) is 1 (pure noise) plus \(N\) times the heritability it tags through LD, where \(r_{jk}^2\) is the squared correlation between SNPs \(j\) and \(k\). SumHer finds the heritability that best fits this across all SNPs.

We proceed in three steps: 1. Format the summary statistics (Height.summaries): As in GWAS7, we keep well-imputed common SNPs (MAF >= 0.01 and INFO >= 0.8) and write the 5 columns LDAK needs: Predictor (SNP ID), A1 (effect allele), A2 (other allele), n (sample size) and Z (the \(Z\)-statistic, effect / SE). Data/Height.QC.gz already contains BETA; for files that only report odds ratios (OR), the code uses \(\log(\text{OR})\) instead. (You do not need to learn the awk code below; it only reformats columns.) 2. Compute the tagging file (ldak --calc-tagging): Using the European reference panel (Data/EUR.QC, whose SNPs match the height GWAS), we thin near-duplicate SNPs as above and calculate how much heritability each SNP tags within 1000 kb. We also save the matrix (tag_ldak.matrix) needed to compute per-SNP heritabilities. 3. Estimate heritability (ldak --sum-hers): Fits the equation above to estimate the total SNP heritability (sumher_ldak.hers) and the expected heritability of each SNP (sumher_ldak.ind.hers).

# Note: this step takes about 1 minute to run.
# 1. Format Data/Height.QC.gz into LDAK summary statistics format (Predictor A1 A2 n Z)
gzip -dc Data/Height.QC.gz | awk '
NR==1 {
    for (i=1; i<=NF; i++) col[$i]=i
    print "Predictor A1 A2 n Z"
    next
}
$col["MAF"] >= 0.01 && $col["INFO"] >= 0.8 && $col["SE"] > 0 && !seen[$col["SNP"]]++ {
    n = ("N" in col) ? $col["N"] : 388028
    beta = ("BETA" in col) ? $col["BETA"] : log($col["OR"])
    print $col["SNP"], $col["A1"], $col["A2"], n, beta / $col["SE"]
}' > Results/GWAS8/Height.summaries

head -n 5 Results/GWAS8/Height.summaries

# 2. Thin the EUR reference panel, then calculate the tagging file and heritability matrix
#    under the LDAK-Thin model (power = -0.25)
ldak --thin Results/GWAS8/thin_eur \
     --bfile Data/EUR.QC \
     --window-prune 0.98 \
     --window-kb 100

awk '{print $1, 1}' Results/GWAS8/thin_eur.in > Results/GWAS8/weights_eur.thin

ldak --calc-tagging Results/GWAS8/tag_ldak \
     --bfile Data/EUR.QC \
     --weights Results/GWAS8/weights_eur.thin \
     --power -0.25 \
     --window-kb 1000

# 3. Estimate SNP heritability from summary statistics
ldak --sum-hers Results/GWAS8/sumher_ldak \
     --summary Results/GWAS8/Height.summaries \
     --tagfile Results/GWAS8/tag_ldak.tagging \
     --matrix Results/GWAS8/tag_ldak.matrix

Let’s look at the heritability estimates from all three analyses. In the .reml and .hers files, focus on: - Her_All: the estimated total SNP heritability (Heritability) and its standard error (SE). - LRT_P (only in .reml): the \(p\)-value for testing \(h^2_{\text{SNP}} = 0\).

What to expect: - REML on the simulated cohort: The true value is \(0.5\). With \(N = 5,000\) the standard error is about \(0.05\), so both models should land close to \(0.5\), with a very small LRT_P. (On the \(N \approx 480\) individuals of Data/EUR.QC, the standard error would be about \(0.85\): the estimate can even be above 1, and LRT_P is not significant.) - SumHer on height: This is a different trait, so do not compare its estimate with \(0.5\). Compare its standard error instead.

cat Results/GWAS8/reml_gcta.reml

echo ""
echo "=== 2. Individual-level REML on SIM (N = 5,000): LDAK-Thin Model (power = -0.25) ==="
cat Results/GWAS8/reml_ldak.reml

echo ""
echo "=== 3. Summary-statistics SumHer on height: LDAK-Thin Model (N = 388,028) ==="

  • Q2: Yes. In our run, REML estimated \(h^2_{\text{SNP}} = 0.49\) (SE \(0.05\)) under the GCTA model and \(0.46\) (SE \(0.05\)) under the LDAK-Thin model, both within one standard error of the true value \(0.5\). The two models agree here because our 50,000 SNPs are spread thinly across the genome; with dense, imputed SNPs their estimates usually differ more. The REML standard error is close to the expected \(316/N \approx 0.06\). SumHer uses summary statistics from \(N = 388,028\) people, so its standard error for height is only about \(0.005\) (\(\hat{h}^2_{\text{SNP}} \approx 0.79\)), about ten times smaller. On the \(N \approx 480\) individuals of Data/EUR.QC, REML would give standard errors of about \(0.85\): the estimate would be compatible with any value between 0 and 1.
  • Q3: For a polygenic trait, SNPs that tag more of the genome tag more causal variants, so their \(\chi^2\) statistics are larger on average: the slope of \(\chi^2\) against tagging measures \(h^2_{\text{SNP}}\). Confounding such as population stratification or cryptic relatedness inflates the \(\chi^2\) of all SNPs by roughly the same amount, whatever their tagging, so it mainly moves the line up (the intercept rises above 1) rather than making it steeper. This is the same idea as LD Score regression.

Conclusion

In this tutorial, we defined SNP heritability (\(h^2_{\text{SNP}}\)), saw how the GCTA (\(\alpha = -1\)) and LDAK (\(\alpha = -0.25\)) heritability models differ in how they treat allele frequency and LD, and estimated \(h^2_{\text{SNP}}\) from individual-level genotypes (ldak --reml) and from GWAS summary statistics (ldak --sum-hers). Next, the polygenic model behind REML (\(\sigma_g^2 K\)) becomes the basis of mixed-model GWAS (GWAS9). Combining summary statistics with an LD reference panel is the key idea behind fine-mapping (GWAS10) and advanced polygenic scores (GWAS11), where we also reuse the per-SNP heritabilities sumher_ldak.ind.hers.

NoteOther take-home messages
  • Heritability limits prediction: A polygenic score built from SNPs cannot explain more variance in a new sample than the trait’s \(h^2_{\text{SNP}}\).
  • Sample size matters: REML (ldak --reml) needs several thousand unrelated individuals for a usefully small standard error (SE \(\approx 316/N\); compare our \(N = 5,000\) cohort with the \(N \approx 480\) EUR.QC sample). SumHer (ldak --sum-hers) instead uses summary statistics from large published GWAS (\(N > 50,000\)) together with a smaller LD reference panel.
  • Binary traits: For a case-control trait, convert the estimate from the observed (0/1) scale to the liability scale by adding --prevalence <K> (the disease prevalence in the population) to ldak --reml, or both --prevalence <K> and --ascertainment <P> (the proportion of cases in the GWAS) to ldak --sum-hers.
