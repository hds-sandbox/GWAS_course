Advanced Polygenic Risk Scores (PRS)

NoteImportant notes for this notebook

In GWAS6 and GWAS7, we built polygenic scores (PRS) with clumping and thresholding (C+T) (PLINK and PRSice-2): keep one SNP per LD region, keep the SNPs below a \(p\)-value threshold, and use their GWAS effect sizes unchanged. In this notebook, we use methods that fit all SNPs together and shrink noisy effect estimates towards zero, using the SNP heritability from GWAS8. We compare two approaches, both in LDAK: 1. From summary statistics: MegaPRS (ldak --mega-prs), similar to LDpred2 and PRS-CS 2. From individual-level data: Bolt-Predict (ldak --bolt) and Elastic-Predict (ldak --elastic), trained on the simulated cohort from GWAS8/GWAS9 (Data/SIM.QC, \(N = 5,000\))

Learning outcomes

  • Contrast C+T (PRSice-2) with methods that fit all SNPs jointly and shrink their effects (MegaPRS, LDpred2, PRS-CS, Bolt-Predict, Elastic-Predict)
  • Explain how the expected heritability of each SNP (\(\mathbb{E}[h_j^2]\)) controls how much its effect is shrunk
  • Build a polygenic score from summary statistics with MegaPRS (ldak --mega-prs)
  • Build polygenic scores from individual-level data with ldak --bolt and ldak --elastic, and evaluate them with the incremental \(R^2\) in R

NoteSome theory

Intuition First: From an “On/Off Switch” (PRSice-2) to a “Volume Knob” (Shrinkage)

C+T (PRSice-2, GWAS6 and GWAS7) works like an on/off switch: - Clumping keeps only the top SNP in each LD region and discards the rest, even if the region has more than one causal variant (GWAS10). - Thresholding uses a SNP’s GWAS effect \(\hat{\beta}_j\) at full size if \(p_j < P_T\), and ignores it (effect 0) otherwise.

Shrinkage methods (MegaPRS, LDpred2, PRS-CS, Bolt-Predict, Elastic-Predict) work like a volume knob: they keep all SNPs, account for the LD between them, and turn each effect down according to how much we trust it. SNPs that are expected to explain little heritability (\(h_j^2\) from GWAS8) and SNPs with noisy estimates are turned down the most.

Why Move Beyond Clumping + Thresholding (C+T)?

A polygenic score for individual \(i\) is \(\text{PRS}_i = \sum_{j=1}^M X_{ij} \tilde{\beta}_j\), where \(\tilde{\beta}_j\) is the weight of SNP \(j\). Shrinkage methods estimate all weights jointly. In the simplest case, the ridge model (each SNP effect is Gaussian with variance \(h_j^2\); standardized genotypes and phenotype), the weights are

\[\tilde{\beta} = \left( R + \frac{1}{N} \text{diag}\!\left(\frac{1}{h_j^2}\right) \right)^{-1} \hat{\beta}_{\text{GWAS}}\]

where \(R\) is the LD matrix and \(N\) the GWAS sample size. Two things happen: multiplying by \(R^{-1}\) removes the double counting of SNPs in LD, and the term \(1/(N h_j^2)\) shrinks the effects. The shrinkage is strong when \(N\) is small (noisy estimates) or \(h_j^2\) is small (SNP not expected to matter).

Summary-Statistics vs. Individual-Level PRS Estimators

Data Used Method Prior on SNP Effects (\(b_j\)) Heritability Model Input Data Required
Summary statistics C+T (PRSice-2) None: \(p\)-value threshold + LD clumping None GWAS summary statistics + LD reference
Summary statistics LDpred2 Point-normal: a fraction \(p\) of SNPs has a Gaussian effect, the rest exactly 0 GCTA (\(\alpha = -1\)) GWAS summary statistics + LD matrix \(R\)
Summary statistics PRS-CS Continuous shrinkage prior (strong shrinkage for small effects, little for large ones) GCTA (\(\alpha = -1\)) GWAS summary statistics + pre-computed LD
Summary statistics MegaPRS (--mega-prs) Choice of ridge (≈ LDpred-inf), bolt (two Gaussians, ≈ LDpred2), lasso or BayesR LDAK (\(\alpha = -0.25\)) or BLD-LDAK GWAS summary statistics + LD correlations (ldak --calc-cors)
Individual-level Bolt-Predict (--bolt) Mixture of two Gaussians (large and small effects) LDAK (\(\alpha = -0.25\)) Genotypes (\(X\)) and phenotype (\(y\))
Individual-level Elastic-Predict (--elastic) Elastic net: mixture of a Laplace (\(L_1\)) and a Gaussian (\(L_2\)) prior LDAK (\(\alpha = -0.25\)) Genotypes (\(X\)) and phenotype (\(y\))

Which one should you use? - Summary statistics (MegaPRS, LDpred2, PRS-CS): when the largest training data available is a published GWAS whose genotypes you cannot access. - Individual-level (Bolt-Predict, Elastic-Predict): when you have the genotypes and phenotypes of a large cohort yourself. Using \(X\) and \(y\) directly avoids the approximations of summary statistics and any mismatch with an LD reference panel (Zhang et al., 2021).

Quick Concept Check (from Lecture 11): - A trait is driven by a few large-effect SNPs, while most SNPs have no effect. Which kind of MegaPRS prior fits best? A sparse prior such as --model bolt (or bayesr), which shrinks most SNPs almost to zero but leaves the large effects nearly untouched. For a highly polygenic trait, --model ridge works as well. - You have genotypes of \(N = 400\) people and a published GWAS of \(N = 250{,}000\). How should you train the PRS? Use MegaPRS on the published summary statistics with an LD reference panel, and keep your 400 people for testing: the training sample size \(N\) is the main driver of PRS accuracy.

Summary-Statistics Based PRS with LDAK MegaPRS

Let’s build a polygenic score for height from the GWAS summary statistics (Data/Height.QC.gz) with MegaPRS: - --calc-cors <prefix>: computes the LD correlations between SNPs within 1 Mb (--window-kb 1000) in the reference panel (Data/EUR.QC). - --mega-prs <prefix>: estimates the SNP weights (<prefix>.effects) from the summary statistics (--summary), the LD correlations (--cors) and the per-SNP heritabilities from GWAS8 (--ind-hers sumher_ldak.ind.hers). With --cv-proportion 0.1, it chooses the best model settings by pseudo-validation: it uses the summary statistics of 90% of the GWAS sample to train and of the other 10% to test. The split is random, so we fix the random seed (--random-seed 42) to make the results close to ours. - --model bolt: a mixture of two Gaussians (the LDAK counterpart of LDpred2). It tries several values of \(p\) (the fraction of SNPs with larger effects) and \(f_2\) (the fraction of heritability they explain), and keeps the best. - --model ridge: a single Gaussian for all SNPs (the LDAK counterpart of LDpred-inf). - --calc-scores <prefix>: multiplies each person’s genotypes by the SNP weights (--scorefile) and adds them up; the scores are written to <prefix>.profile. --power 0 tells LDAK that the weights are per-allele effects that should be used as they are.

Switch to the Bash kernel.

Let’s link the Data folder, create Results/GWAS11, and run MegaPRS:

# Note: this step takes about 1 minute to run.
ln -sf ../Data
mkdir -p Results/GWAS11

# Prepare target phenotype and covariates for LDAK
awk 'NR>1 {print $1, $2, $3}' Data/EUR.height > Results/GWAS11/EUR.pheno
awk 'NR>1 {print $0}' Data/EUR.covariate > Results/GWAS11/EUR.covar

# Reuse Height.summaries and sumher_ldak.ind.hers from GWAS8 if present; otherwise create them
if [ -f Results/GWAS8/sumher_ldak.ind.hers ] && [ -f Results/GWAS8/Height.summaries ]; then
  cp Results/GWAS8/Height.summaries Results/GWAS11/Height.summaries
  cp Results/GWAS8/sumher_ldak.ind.hers Results/GWAS11/sumher_ldak.ind.hers
else
  gzip -dc Data/Height.QC.gz | awk '
  NR==1 {
    for (i=1; i<=NF; i++) col[$i]=i
    print "Predictor A1 A2 n Z"
    next
  }
  $col["MAF"] >= 0.01 && $col["INFO"] >= 0.8 && $col["SE"] > 0 && !seen[$col["SNP"]]++ {
    n = ("N" in col) ? $col["N"] : 388028
    beta = ("BETA" in col) ? $col["BETA"] : log($col["OR"])
    print $col["SNP"], $col["A1"], $col["A2"], n, beta / $col["SE"]
  }' > Results/GWAS11/Height.summaries
  ldak --thin Results/GWAS11/thin --bfile Data/EUR.QC --window-prune 0.98 --window-kb 100
  awk '{print $1, 1}' Results/GWAS11/thin.in > Results/GWAS11/weights.thin
  ldak --calc-tagging Results/GWAS11/tag_ldak --bfile Data/EUR.QC --weights Results/GWAS11/weights.thin --power -0.25 --window-kb 1000
  ldak --sum-hers Results/GWAS11/sumher_ldak --summary Results/GWAS11/Height.summaries --tagfile Results/GWAS11/tag_ldak.tagging --matrix Results/GWAS11/tag_ldak.matrix
fi

# 1. Compute local LD correlation windows (1000 kb) for MegaPRS
ldak \
  --calc-cors Results/GWAS11/cors_eur \
  --bfile Data/EUR.QC \
  --window-kb 1000

# 2. Run MegaPRS with Bolt (LDpred2 spike-and-slab) and Ridge (LDpred-inf) priors
ldak \
  --mega-prs Results/GWAS11/megaprs_bolt \
  --model bolt \
  --ind-hers Results/GWAS11/sumher_ldak.ind.hers \
  --summary Results/GWAS11/Height.summaries \
  --cors Results/GWAS11/cors_eur \
  --cv-proportion 0.1 \
  --random-seed 42

ldak \
  --mega-prs Results/GWAS11/megaprs_ridge \
  --model ridge \
  --ind-hers Results/GWAS11/sumher_ldak.ind.hers \
  --summary Results/GWAS11/Height.summaries \
  --cors Results/GWAS11/cors_eur \
  --cv-proportion 0.1 \
  --random-seed 42

# 3. Score target individuals in Data/EUR.QC
ldak --calc-scores Results/GWAS11/prs_megaprs_bolt --bfile Data/EUR.QC --scorefile Results/GWAS11/megaprs_bolt.effects --power 0 --pheno Results/GWAS11/EUR.pheno
ldak --calc-scores Results/GWAS11/prs_megaprs_ridge --bfile Data/EUR.QC --scorefile Results/GWAS11/megaprs_ridge.effects --power 0 --pheno Results/GWAS11/EUR.pheno
NotePseudo-validation estimates

ldak --mega-prs prints the estimated accuracy (“approximate correlation”) of the best model; the values for all models are in megaprs_bolt.cors. In our run, the best Bolt model had an approximate correlation of about \(1.3\), which is impossible for a real correlation, while its actual correlation with height in Data/EUR.QC (printed by --calc-scores) was only about \(0.40\). Pseudo-validation depends on the LD reference panel, and ours has only ~480 individuals (who are also the people we score), so its estimates are unreliable. Always check a PRS in a real target sample.

Also look at which Bolt model was chosen (megaprs_bolt.best and megaprs_bolt.cors). Pseudo-validation picked a sparse model (\(p = 0.02\): 2% of the SNPs explain half of the heritability), so most SNPs are shrunk strongly towards zero. Height is highly polygenic, and the Ridge model, which gives every SNP a small effect, predicts height better (incremental \(R^2 \approx 0.23\) vs. \(0.16\) for Bolt). With a small LD panel, the “best” model by pseudo-validation is not necessarily the best model in reality.

Individual-Level PRS with LDAK-Bolt-Predict and LDAK-Elastic-Predict

Now suppose we have the genotypes \(X\) and phenotypes \(y\) of a cohort ourselves. With the ~480 individuals of Data/EUR.QC, individual-level scores explain almost nothing (incremental \(R^2 < 0.01\)), so we use the simulated cohort Data/SIM.QC (\(N = 5,000\); true \(h^2_{\text{SNP}} = 0.5\)). We: 1. Split it into a training set of 80% (train.keep, \(N = 4,000\)) and a test set of 20% (test.keep, \(N = 1,000\)). 2. Estimate the SNP heritability in the training set (--reml). 3. Train two prediction models on the training set. --LOCO NO asks for one genome-wide model (*.effects) instead of 22 leave-one-chromosome-out models as in GWAS9: - --bolt <prefix>: Bolt-Predict (mixture of two Gaussians), using the LDAK model (--power -0.25). (The per-SNP heritabilities sumher_ldak.ind.hers from GWAS8 belong to the height GWAS SNPs, so we cannot use them here.) - --elastic <prefix>: Elastic-Predict (elastic net; the same model as Step 1 of LDAK-KVIK). 4. Compute the scores of the test set with ldak --calc-scores (--keep Results/GWAS11/test.keep).

# Note: this step takes about 2 minutes to run.
# Prepare the simulated phenotype (true h2_SNP = 0.5) and covariates for LDAK
awk 'NR>1 {print $1, $2, $3}' Data/SIM.pheno > Results/GWAS11/SIM.pheno
awk 'NR>1 {print $0}' Data/SIM.covariate > Results/GWAS11/SIM.covar

# 1. Create an 80% Training / 20% Testing split
awk 'NR % 5 != 0 {print $1, $2}' Results/GWAS11/SIM.pheno > Results/GWAS11/train.keep
awk 'NR % 5 == 0 {print $1, $2}' Results/GWAS11/SIM.pheno > Results/GWAS11/test.keep

# 2. Estimate SNP heritability on the 80% Training set using REML
ldak --calc-kins-direct Results/GWAS11/kin_train --bfile Data/SIM.QC --keep Results/GWAS11/train.keep --ignore-weights YES --power -0.25
ldak --reml Results/GWAS11/reml_train --grm Results/GWAS11/kin_train --pheno Results/GWAS11/SIM.pheno --covar Results/GWAS11/SIM.covar --keep Results/GWAS11/train.keep
grep Her_All Results/GWAS11/reml_train.reml

# 3. Fit Individual-Level Bolt-Predict (--bolt) and Elastic-Predict (--elastic) on Training set
ldak \
  --bolt Results/GWAS11/ind_bolt \
  --bfile Data/SIM.QC \
  --pheno Results/GWAS11/SIM.pheno \
  --covar Results/GWAS11/SIM.covar \
  --keep Results/GWAS11/train.keep \
  --power -0.25 \
  --cv-proportion 0.1 \
  --random-seed 42 \
  --LOCO NO

ldak \
  --elastic Results/GWAS11/ind_elastic \
  --bfile Data/SIM.QC \
  --pheno Results/GWAS11/SIM.pheno \
  --covar Results/GWAS11/SIM.covar \
  --keep Results/GWAS11/train.keep \
  --power -0.25 \
  --cv-proportion 0.1 \
  --random-seed 42 \
  --LOCO NO

# 4. Project trained weights onto the held-out 20% Test set
ldak --calc-scores Results/GWAS11/prs_ind_bolt --bfile Data/SIM.QC --keep Results/GWAS11/test.keep --scorefile Results/GWAS11/ind_bolt.effects --power 0 --pheno Results/GWAS11/SIM.pheno
ldak --calc-scores Results/GWAS11/prs_ind_elastic --bfile Data/SIM.QC --keep Results/GWAS11/test.keep --scorefile Results/GWAS11/ind_elastic.effects --power 0 --pheno Results/GWAS11/SIM.pheno

Evaluating PRS Accuracy in R

Switch to the R kernel.

As in GWAS6 and GWAS7, we measure accuracy with the incremental \(R^2\): how much more of the phenotypic variance is explained when we add the standardized score (Profile_1) to a model with only the covariates (Phenotype ~ Sex + PC1 + ... + PC6). The MegaPRS scores are evaluated for height in Data/EUR.QC, and the individual-level scores for the simulated trait in the 1,000 test individuals of Data/SIM.QC.

# Setup to avoid long messages and plot on screen
options(warn = -1)

# Helper function to compute incremental R2 over Sex + PC1..PC6
# Note: ldak --calc-scores outputs .profile with columns ID1, ID2, Phenotype, Covariates, Profile_1
calc_incremental_r2 <- function(profile_file, pheno_df, covar_df) {
  prs <- read.table(profile_file, header = TRUE)
  colnames(prs)[1:2] <- c("FID", "IID")
  colnames(pheno_df)[3] <- "Phenotype"
  dat <- merge(pheno_df, covar_df, by = c("FID", "IID"))
  dat <- merge(dat, prs[, c("FID", "IID", "Profile_1")], by = c("FID", "IID"))
  dat$PRS_std <- as.numeric(scale(dat$Profile_1))
  
  null_fit <- lm(Phenotype ~ Sex + PC1 + PC2 + PC3 + PC4 + PC5 + PC6, data = dat)
  full_fit <- lm(Phenotype ~ PRS_std + Sex + PC1 + PC2 + PC3 + PC4 + PC5 + PC6, data = dat)
  
  list(
    inc_r2 = summary(full_fit)$r.squared - summary(null_fit)$r.squared,
    p_val  = summary(full_fit)$coefficients["PRS_std", "Pr(>|t|)"],
    data   = dat
  )
}

pheno     <- read.table("Data/EUR.height", header = TRUE)
covar     <- read.table("Data/EUR.covariate", header = TRUE)
sim_pheno <- read.table("Data/SIM.pheno", header = TRUE)
sim_covar <- read.table("Data/SIM.covariate", header = TRUE)
n_train   <- length(readLines("Results/GWAS11/train.keep"))   # size of the individual-level training set
ind_label <- paste0("(SIM, N_train=", format(n_train, big.mark = ","), ")")

res_mega_bolt  <- calc_incremental_r2("Results/GWAS11/prs_megaprs_bolt.profile", pheno, covar)
res_mega_ridge <- calc_incremental_r2("Results/GWAS11/prs_megaprs_ridge.profile", pheno, covar)
res_ind_bolt   <- calc_incremental_r2("Results/GWAS11/prs_ind_bolt.profile", sim_pheno, sim_covar)
res_ind_elas   <- calc_incremental_r2("Results/GWAS11/prs_ind_elastic.profile", sim_pheno, sim_covar)

cat("Summary-Stats MegaPRS (Bolt / LDpred2) Incremental R2:     ", round(res_mega_bolt$inc_r2, 4), "(p =", signif(res_mega_bolt$p_val, 3), ")\n")
cat("Summary-Stats MegaPRS (Ridge / LDpred-inf) Inc. R2:        ", round(res_mega_ridge$inc_r2, 4), "(p =", signif(res_mega_ridge$p_val, 3), ")\n")
cat("Individual-Level LDAK-Bolt", ind_label, "Inc. R2:   ", round(res_ind_bolt$inc_r2, 4), "(p =", signif(res_ind_bolt$p_val, 3), ")\n")
cat("Individual-Level LDAK-Elastic", ind_label, "Inc. R2:", round(res_ind_elas$inc_r2, 4), "(p =", signif(res_ind_elas$p_val, 3), ")\n")

  • Q1: The MegaPRS scores explain a substantial part of the variance in height beyond the covariates: about 23% for Ridge (incremental \(R^2 \approx 0.23\)) but only about 16% for Bolt (\(R^2 \approx 0.16\)). Pseudo-validation chose a sparse Bolt model (see the note above), which suits traits with some large effects; height is highly polygenic, so the Ridge prior does better. The individual-level Bolt-Predict and Elastic-Predict scores explain about 13% of the variance of the simulated trait in the 1,000 test individuals (\(p < 10^{-30}\)).
  • Q2: The individual-level scores reach about a quarter of the maximum \(h^2_{\text{SNP}} = 0.5\) (\(R^2 \approx 0.13\); reml_train estimates \(h^2_{\text{SNP}} \approx 0.45\) in the training set). Following the formula, accuracy depends on how \(N h^2_{\text{SNP}}\) compares with \(M_e\):
    • With \(N \approx 380\), \(N h^2_{\text{SNP}}\) is only a few hundred, far below \(M_e\) (tens of thousands for genome-wide data), so the estimated effects are mostly noise and are shrunk almost to zero.
    • With \(N = 4,000\) we do much better, partly because the simulated trait is easy: only 1,000 causal SNPs, all of them among the 50,000 genotyped SNPs, which the sparse Bolt/Elastic priors can find. Real traits such as height have many more causal variants with tiny effects and need far larger samples.
    • The height MegaPRS was trained on \(N = 388{,}028\), so it is limited less by \(N\) than by the small LD reference panel and the differences between the GWAS and target samples.
    • When both approaches use the same large sample (as in the UK Biobank), individual-level methods usually win, because they use the exact LD of the sample instead of approximating it (Zhang et al., 2021).

Conclusion

In this tutorial, we brought the advanced part of the course together: the per-SNP heritabilities from GWAS8 (sumher_ldak.ind.hers), the GWAS summary statistics (Data/Height.QC.gz) and the LD correlations of a reference panel (ldak --calc-cors, the genome-wide version of the LD matrix used in GWAS10) to build a polygenic score with MegaPRS. We compared it with scores trained on individual-level data (ldak --bolt and ldak --elastic; --elastic is the same model as Step 1 of LDAK-KVIK in GWAS9).

NoteOther take-home messages
  • Shrinkage beats clumping and thresholding: MegaPRS, LDpred2 and PRS-CS keep correlated SNPs and shrink noisy GWAS estimates using the SNP heritability, instead of switching SNPs on or off.
  • Choose the method that fits your data: use ldak --mega-prs with large published GWAS summary statistics, and ldak --bolt or ldak --elastic when you have the genotypes of a large cohort yourself.
  • Heritability links the whole advanced course: we estimated \(h^2_{\text{SNP}}\) (GWAS8), used it to predict the genetic background in mixed-model GWAS (GWAS9), met the same idea as the effect-size prior in fine-mapping (GWAS10), and used it to shrink polygenic score weights (GWAS11).
